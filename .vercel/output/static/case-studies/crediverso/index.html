Case Study 03

Crediverso

A bilingual bank where family is a feature, not a footnote.

Role
Designer on a three-person team. Owned onboarding and credit card flows.
Timeline
February 2021 to November 2022
Tools
Figma, Jira, Google Analytics
Tags
Fintech · Mobile · Bilingual · Shipped Product

Context

Crediverso is a bilingual fintech serving the US Hispanic community. In 2020 it became the first US company to offer credit checks in Spanish for free.

I joined in early 2021 doing marketing and graphic design. Eight months in, when the company decided to build a consumer mobile banking app, I moved onto the design team: three of us total, a product designer, me, and an external graphic designer handling overflow. We collaborated intensely on the system, and I owned end-to-end design on the onboarding and credit card flows through the iOS launch 14 months later.

The mandate: ship V1 of an all-in-one bilingual banking product in a market that had been failed by fragmented, English-only financial tooling.

The Problem

US Hispanic consumers face structural barriers in consumer finance: language gaps in primary product flows, fragmented multi-product experiences, and a lack of family-aware products that match how Hispanic households actually move money.

The existing mobile UI lacked functionality and felt dated compared to competitors.

Three product goals anchored V1:

  • One account, every product. Users engage multiple partners without leaving the app.
  • Bilingual at every step. Not a translation layer, a primary language requirement.
  • Family-aware money movement. Virtual cash sharing across household members.

Customer Insights

We synthesized Zoom interviews, web analytics, social listening, and competitor analyses into three customer avatars we referenced through every sprint.

Three findings drove direction:

  • 79% of US Hispanics consider sending money home important, and 75% use remittance services. Remittance is a primary banking use case, not an edge feature.
  • 41 million Americans are Spanish-dominant, and preference for Spanish rises sharply when financial terminology gets complex. Bilingual is a core flow requirement, not a translation pass.
  • 81% of surveyed Hispanics said relatives less fluent in English asked them to research purchases on their behalf. Financial decisions in this market are family-coordinated, not individual.

Decisions

One account, all the products. Existing marketplaces stop after a user selects a third-party product. Crediverso owned the entire value chain: application, transaction, cross-product flow through the same account, discovery of new products through the same identity. We built Figma primitives around this multi-product structure so the same patterns could serve any new partner without redesign.

Family as a first-class primitive. Family Cards turned household structure into a product object. Each family member gets a card. Funds move between cards. The primary user (Juan, our principal avatar) sees his account and the family group at the same altitude on the home screen. Family Cards became a primitive that other partner-product surfaces inherited as the app grew.

The nav-bar SWOT. A V1 debate I kept coming back to: split primary and family accounts across separate tabs, or combine them on a single dashboard? We ran SWOTs both ways:

  • Split: clearer mental model per context, lower screen density, more taps to move money.
  • Combined: denser home screen, more layout complexity, fewer taps for the primary action.

We chose combined. The product is family-first by identity, and splitting it would have diluted the thesis. The Move Money flow became a dedicated action surface inside the combined Accounts tab.

What Shipped

V1 launched on iOS in November 2022 with full bilingual coverage across every flow.

  • Usability testing with 50+ participants validated the primary flows and dropped primary-flow bounce by 15%.
  • Pre-launch organic growth from A/B-tested messaging grew the channel from 700 to 2,100 followers (125%) before App Store release.

Reflections

The nav-bar decision is the one I keep coming back to. Two equally defensible SWOTs led to a non-obvious choice that only resolved once we put the product thesis (family-first identity) in front of the screen-density question. The pattern carries forward: when SWOTs resolve evenly, the tiebreaker is rarely "best UX," it's "which option reinforces the product's identity."

Bilingual is worth a second pass. Treating Spanish as a translation layer would have shipped a product that worked in two languages but felt designed in one. Treating bilingual as a core requirement, not a switch, shaped how every screen's information hierarchy got built. That principle generalizes to any product serving a multi-context audience.
